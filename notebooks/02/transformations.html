2.2. Annual growth: average-over-average vs Q4/Q4#

Annual GDP growth in the press is the growth of the annual average. Policy forecasts (e.g. FOMC projections) use Q4 over Q4. They can differ by more than a percentage point.

annual = gdp.groupby(gdp.index.year).mean()  # SAAR levels: average them
avg_growth = 100 * annual.pct_change()
q4q4 = 100 * gdp[gdp.index.quarter == 4].pct_change()
q4q4.index = q4q4.index.year
print(pd.DataFrame({"avg/avg": avg_growth, "Q4/Q4": q4q4}).loc[2019:2025].round(1))

2.3. Carry-over (statistical overhang)#

How much annual growth is “already in the bag” if the series stays flat at its last quarter for the rest of the year? It is the last quarter’s level relative to the previous year’s average.

def carry_over(q: pd.Series, year: int) -> float:
    """Annual growth in `year` if the series stays at its Q4 level of year-1."""
    prev = q[q.index.year == year - 1]
    return 100 * (prev.iloc[-1] / prev.mean() - 1)


print(f"carry-over into 2026: {carry_over(gdp, 2026):.2f}%")

carry-over into 2026: 0.76%

In your projects

The same recipe is in the econts toolkit:

from econts.transform import carry_over

2.4. Rebasing an index#

ip = load("INDPRO")
ip_2015 = 100 * ip / ip["2015"].mean()  # 2015 average = 100
print(ip_2015["2015"].mean().round(6), ip_2015.iloc[-1].round(1))

100.0 102.9

Rebasing a single series is harmless. Note that chain-linked volumes are not additive in any reference year: real components do not sum to real GDP, so compute contributions to growth from the components’ own growth rates and shares, not from differences in levels.

2.5. Nominal to real: deflating#

Real = nominal / price index × 100, with the price index based to 100 in the reference year of the real series.

ngdp = load("GDP").to_period("Q")
defl = load("GDPDEF").to_period("Q")
real = ngdp / defl * 100  # deflator is 2017 = 100
check = pd.DataFrame({"implied": real, "published": gdp}).dropna()
print((check["implied"] / check["published"] - 1).abs().max())  # < 1e-4: rounding only

4.058027189679514e-05

Deflate with the price index that matches the aggregate (wages by CPI for purchasing power, output by its own deflator). For mixed frequencies, first bring the deflator to the frequency of the nominal series (chapter 3).

2.6. Demeaning, standardizing and scaling#

Demeaning subtracts a mean; which mean is the decision. The full-sample mean (EMT demean) is the default; pandas skips NaN when computing it.

g_ip = 100 * np.log(ip).diff()  # monthly IP growth, %
demeaned = g_ip - g_ip.mean()  # full-sample mean
print(round(g_ip.mean(), 3), round(demeaned.mean(), 12))

0.237

 -0.0

By regime. Trend growth was higher before 2000. Demeaning each regime by its own mean removes the shift that a single mean would leave in:

regime = np.where(g_ip.index < "2000-01-01", "pre-2000", "2000+")
print(g_ip.groupby(regime).mean().round(3))
by_regime = g_ip - g_ip.groupby(regime).transform("mean")

2000+       0.039
pre-2000    0.302
Name: INDPRO, dtype: float64

By calendar month. Subtracting each month’s average growth removes a stable seasonal pattern: a crude seasonal adjustment (chapter 5 does it properly).

g_nsa = 100 * np.log(load("RSXFSN")).diff()
by_month = g_nsa - g_nsa.groupby(g_nsa.index.month).transform("mean")
print(g_nsa.std().round(2), "->", by_month.std().round(2))  # most variance was seasonal

10.08 -> 2.89

By unit, in a panel (the within transformation). With several countries in long format, subtract each country’s mean: this is what a fixed-effects regression does. Subtracting each period’s cross-country mean as well gives the two-way transformation.

unemp = (
    pd.concat(
        {
            "US": load("UNRATE").to_period("M").resample("Q").mean(),
            "PT": load("LRHUTTTTPTQ156S").to_period("Q"),
        },
        names=["country", "date"],
    )
    .rename("u")
    .loc[:, "1983":"2019"]
    .reset_index()
)

unemp["within"] = unemp["u"] - unemp.groupby("country")["u"].transform("mean")
unemp["two_way"] = unemp["within"] - unemp.groupby("date")["within"].transform("mean")
print(unemp.groupby("country")[["u", "within"]].mean().round(2))

Standardizing divides the demeaned series by its standard deviation (EMT standard), so series in different units can be compared or combined (as the factor model of chapter 8 does):

def zscore(s: pd.Series) -> pd.Series:
    return (s - s.mean()) / s.std()


z = zscore(100 * np.log(gdp).diff().dropna())

Demean and standardize on the estimation sample only; reusing full-sample moments in a forecasting exercise leaks future information (chapter 7’s expanding() gives the real-time version). Demeaning a series with a trend does not make it stationary: detrend it instead (chapter 6).

2.7. Splicing series with a level break#

Statistical offices rebase and redefine series. To build one long series, splice the old one onto the new one using growth rates of the old series (ratio splicing). Here we fake a rebased “old” industrial production index.

old = (1.37 * ip[:"2012"]).rename("old")  # old base, ends 2012
new = ip["2005":].rename("new")  # new base, starts 2005


def ratio_splice(old: pd.Series, new: pd.Series, at=None) -> pd.Series:
    """Rescale `old` to the level of `new` at the link date and join them."""
    at = at or new.index[0]
    factor = new[at] / old[at]
    return pd.concat([old[old.index < at] * factor, new[new.index >= at]])


long = ratio_splice(old, new)
print((long / ip - 1).abs().max())  # 0: growth rates of old preserved

2.220446049250313e-16

In your projects

The same recipe is in the econts toolkit:

from econts.transform import ratio_splice

Link at a single date, or at an average over the overlap (new[overlap].mean() / old[overlap].mean()) when either series is noisy. For differences in levels (e.g. a percentage), splice additively instead.

2.8. Pitfalls#

  • Missing observations. BLS never published the October 2025 CPI (a federal government shutdown), so CPIAUCSL has a NaN there. pandas 3 returns NaN for any growth rate that touches it; pandas 2 by default fills the gap with the previous value and reports a spurious 0% m/m change. Pass fill_method=None so the code behaves the same in both.

  • pct_change on a series with dropped rows compares across the gap. Call .asfreq(...) first so that the gap produces NaN (chapter 1).

  • Logs of zero or negative numbers (inventories change, net exports) are undefined; use differences scaled by a level instead (contributions).

  • Percent vs percentage point: unemployment going from 4% to 5% is a 1 pp rise and a 25% increase.

2.9. Summary#

  • Work in logs × 100 for anything that grows: differences are then (approximately) percent changes and trends become linear.

  • Say which growth rate you report: q/q, q/q annualized (SAAR), y/y, or Q4/Q4. They answer different questions.

  • US national accounts levels are already annualized (SAAR). Divide by 4 for the quarterly level; average, don’t sum, to get annual values.

2.10. Exercises#

  1. Compute q/q, annualized q/q, y/y and 400 × Δlog growth of real GDP (GDPC1) for 2008–2010. In which quarter does each measure first turn negative, and in which does it turn positive again? Why does y/y lag the other two?

  2. Compute average-over-average and Q4/Q4 real GDP growth for 2007–2011. Use carry_over to explain why the two measures tell such different stories about 2009.

  3. Build real retail sales as RSXFS / CPIAUCSL, rebased so that the 2019 average is 100. Compare the y/y growth of nominal and real retail sales in 2021 and 2022: how much of the nominal growth was price increases?

  4. Demean monthly industrial production growth (INDPRO, 1960–2019) once with the full-sample mean and once by regime (before and after 2000). Compute the first-order autocorrelation of each and explain why a single mean can overstate persistence when the mean shifts.
